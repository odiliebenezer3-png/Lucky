Picture-in-Picture & Layers

Layering multiple clips on screen at once opens up reaction shots, split-screen comparisons, and richer compositions. This guide covers how to build clean picture-in-picture layouts and manage multiple layers without the result looking cluttered.

1. Adding a picture-in-picture layer

In CapCut, tap the "+" (Add) button while your main clip is on the timeline, and import a second clip — it'll automatically stack as an overlay layer above your base track. Drag the overlay clip on the preview canvas to reposition it, and use the corner handles to resize it into a smaller inset frame.

2. Framing your overlay intentionally

Don't just drop the overlay wherever it lands by default — consider what's happening in the base layer beneath it. Position the overlay over visually quiet areas of the frame (like empty background space) rather than directly over a subject's face or the main action, so both layers stay readable at once.

3. Adding borders and shadows for separation

A picture-in-picture layer without any visual separation can blend awkwardly into the base footage. In CapCut, select the overlay clip, go to the Edit panel, and add a border/stroke (a thin white or colored outline) plus a drop shadow. This small touch makes it immediately clear to viewers that they're looking at two distinct layers.

4. Building split-screen layouts

For side-by-side comparisons, resize and reposition two clips to each occupy half the frame rather than using a small inset. Use the scale and position tools to align both clips precisely — CapCut shows alignment guides (dotted lines) when a clip is centered or edge-aligned, which helps keep your split clean and symmetrical.

5. Managing more than two layers

Once you're stacking three or more layers (like a main shot, a reaction cam, and a text overlay), organization matters. Rename your layers if possible, and think about z-order — which layer sits on top of which — since overlapping layers will hide whatever's beneath them. Keep your most important visual element on the topmost layer, and use lower layers for background or supporting footage.

6. Syncing multiple layers to the same audio

When compositing multiple camera angles or reaction shots that occurred at the same time, sync them to a shared audio cue (like a clap or a distinct sound in the original take) so movements across layers line up naturally. Zoom into the timeline and align the audio waveforms of each clip as closely as possible before locking the layout in place.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Overlapping important content: placing an overlay directly over key action in the base layer competes for attention.
  • No visual separation: layers without borders or shadows can blend together and confuse the viewer.
  • Unsynced layers: multi-camera or reaction layers that drift out of sync feel unpolished, even by a few frames.
