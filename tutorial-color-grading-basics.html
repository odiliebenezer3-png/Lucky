Color Grading Basics

Color grading is what gives an edit its mood and consistency — the difference between clips that look like they were shot on different days and a sequence that feels intentional from start to finish. This guide covers curves, LUTs, and color wheels, the three core tools CapCut offers for shaping your look.

1. Color correction before color grading

Before applying any stylistic look, fix the basics: exposure, white balance, and contrast. In CapCut, tap your clip, go to Adjust, and correct brightness, contrast, and temperature so skin tones look natural and the footage isn't over- or under-exposed. Grading on top of poorly corrected footage will only amplify the problems.

2. Understanding curves

The Curves tool (under Adjust → Curves) lets you control brightness and color independently across the shadows, midtones, and highlights of your image. Dragging the bottom-left point up lifts your blacks for a faded, filmic look. Dragging the top-right point down softens your highlights, which is common in moody, cinematic grades. Small adjustments go a long way — subtle S-curves (slight dip in shadows, slight lift in highlights) add contrast without looking artificial.

3. Using LUTs as a starting point

A LUT (Look-Up Table) applies a preset color transformation to your footage in one tap. In CapCut, go to Effects → Filters or the dedicated LUT import option, and apply a LUT to see an instant style shift. Treat LUTs as a starting point, not a finished result — footage shot in different lighting will react differently to the same LUT, so plan to adjust opacity or fine-tune curves afterward rather than applying it at full strength and moving on.

4. Working with color wheels

Color wheels (Shadows, Midtones, Highlights) let you push specific tonal ranges toward a particular color — a technique behind the popular teal-and-orange cinematic look, where shadows are pushed toward teal/blue and highlights toward warm orange. In CapCut, access these under Adjust → HSL or the dedicated color wheel panel, and nudge each wheel slightly rather than dragging to the extreme — subtlety reads as intentional, while heavy-handed color pushes read as a filter.

5. Keeping skin tones natural

Aggressive color grades can easily push skin tones toward orange, green, or red in ways that look unnatural. After applying a grade, zoom into any shots with people and check that skin still looks believable. If it's shifted too far, dial back the midtone color wheel slightly or use HSL adjustments to isolate and correct the orange/red hue range specifically.

6. Keeping your grade consistent across clips

Once you find a grade you like on one clip, copy those adjustment settings to the rest of your footage rather than manually recreating them. In CapCut, use the "Copy Settings" option (long-press the graded clip → Copy → paste onto other clips) to apply the same adjustments, then fine-tune individual clips slightly if lighting conditions varied during the shoot.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Grading before correcting: stylizing uncorrected footage locks in exposure and white balance problems.
  • Over-saturating: pushing saturation too far makes footage look artificial rather than cinematic.
  • Inconsistent grades across clips: forgetting to copy settings between clips shot in similar conditions breaks visual continuity.

Once your color grade feels consistent and intentional, the next step is layering in sound design to match the visual mood — covered in the next tutorial.
